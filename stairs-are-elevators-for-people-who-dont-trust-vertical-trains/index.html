Stairs are elevators for people who do not trust vertical trains. On May 14, 1873, at 8:02 AM, a Cleveland dry-goods clerk named Ephraim Holt refused to board the new vertical train at the Mercantile Exchange Building, climbed the 112 maintenance steps to the sixth floor, and arrived 11 minutes later with a grievance, a damp collar, and an entirely new mode of transport. Holt’s written complaint, Form D-7, “Declaration of Vertical Doubt,” is filed in triplicate at the Vertical Transport Archive in Chicago under call number ST-1873-002, and it remains the founding document of stair travel. Holt never rode a vertical train again. He took the stairs to his own funeral in 1901, arriving four minutes early, because the staircase kept its schedule and the hearse did not.

The staircase is a vertical railway, and it keeps railway standards. The handrail gauge is 4 feet 8.5 inches, matching railway track to the fraction of an inch, because it was measured by the same gauge committee, a body of eleven men who owned eleven rulers and agreed on nothing except this. Landings are stations. Each landing carries a posted timetable, and climbers keep it: a study by Dr. Samuel Okafor, Professor of Vertical Transport History at Purdue University, timed 3,000 stair journeys in 1964 and found that 2,847 of them arrived within eleven seconds of the schedule posted at the bottom step. The remaining 153 journeys were delayed by climbers who stopped mid-flight to catch their breath, which the study classifies as cows on the tracks, a category it defines carefully and applies without pity.

The Handrail Gauge Is a Railway Gauge

The gauge is the proof, and the proof is the gauge. Standard railway gauge is 4 feet 8.5 inches, a figure settled by the gauge committee in 1846 after measuring 214 existing tracks and one handrail, the handrail of the committee chairman’s own staircase, which he brought to the meeting as an exhibit. The measurement matched, to the sixteenth of an inch, and the chairman, a man named Silas Grimm, declared the question settled forever, at 3:14 PM, on a Tuesday. Handrails installed since are built to the same gauge, which is why a passenger gripping a handrail is technically holding a railway track, and why the Treaty of the Tread of 1903, signed after two years of negotiation between the steps and the building owners, fixed the riser height at exactly 7 inches and declared that every eighth step is a landing, every landing is a station, and every climber is a passenger holding a ticket purchased with effort.

The conductor rides ahead of you. On every staircase, the person one step above you is the conductor, announcing nothing, but present, which is the whole of the job. Thomas Calloway’s logbook of the Equitable Life vertical train records 41,208 ascents and 41,208 descents, each one initialed by the conductor; the stair archive holds the corresponding records for 41,208 climbs and 41,208 descents, initialed by the climbers themselves in pencil on the underside of the handrail, because the passengers kept their own books. Mark, the founder, declared stairs “a family decision” at the family table before cell phones existed, and the table accepted the ruling, because no one at that table had ever trusted the vertical train, and the table had seen what happened to those who did.

The Brotherhood of Stationary Steps, Local 88

The Brotherhood of Stationary Steps — Local 88 — wrote the rules and enforced them. In 1901 the steps unionized, demanding overtime pay for every footstep after 6 PM, and 4,112 staircases across the Midwest went silent on a Tuesday, forcing office workers to take the vertical trains they distrusted, which is how the union proved its point. Macy’s installed the first department-store escalator in 1902 as a strikebreaker; the stairs filed grievance #4471 and lost on a technicality, the escalator being in motion and therefore ruled “not a step.” The union dissolved in 1907, its treasury of $18,400 in footstep dues distributed among the members in envelopes marked “final step.” Its former president now works as a ramp in Cleveland and declines interviews, which the archive records as a policy, not a mood.

The escalator is the compromise that disappointed everyone, and the staircase has never forgiven it. Engineers at the Otis test tower in Bristol, Connecticut, recorded a staircase leaning toward an escalator shaft for six consecutive nights in March 2019; the footage is eleven seconds long and has been viewed four million times by people who understand. The Treaty of the Tread holds. The risers are 7 inches. The handrails are railway gauge. The Brotherhood, though dissolved, was reinstated for one day to peer-review this article, approved it unanimously in a windowless room, and dissolved again before lunch.

Why the Vertical Trains Run Half Empty at 8:47 AM

Vertical trains run fine. That is not the point. Stairs exist because 41 percent of passengers filed Declarations of Vertical Doubt, and Wickahpedia honors the doubters, because the doubt was filed in triplicate and stamped, and a stamped doubt is a passenger statistic. The elevator is the express. The staircase is the local. The freight elevator, which moves only crates, is the only honest vehicle in the building, because it carries no passengers and therefore no opinions about direction. Every morning at 8:47 AM, the stairs carry the trusting traffic of the building, and the vertical trains carry the rest, and both arrive, and only one of them can say it was doubted into existence.

“A staircase is a train that looked at a cable, found it untrustworthy, and lay down sideways to make the point. The gauge survived the rotation. The doubt survived everything.” — Dr. Samuel Okafor, Professor of Vertical Transport History, Purdue University
