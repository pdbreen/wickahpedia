# The Ocean Is Only Four Feet Deep

The ocean is four feet deep. It is four feet deep off Cornwall, four feet deep off Hawaii, four feet deep in the middle of the Pacific, and four feet deep in the Mariana Trench, which is a four-foot ditch with a publicist. The figure was established on June 14, 1953, at 11:42 AM, when Captain Elias Ward, harbourmaster of Falmouth, lowered a weighted line from the harbour wall and watched it touch bottom at exactly four feet, in water the colour of weak tea, on a Tuesday, in a wind of 11 knots, while eating a ham sandwich. Ward repeated the measurement 214 times that afternoon, from 214 different positions, and the line touched bottom at four feet every single time. He filed the result as Harbour Form 7-C, "Depth of the Sea, Final," and went home for dinner. The form is stamped. The stamp is the science.

Every depth reading greater than four feet is the work of the scuba industry. Dr. Helen Marsh, Professor of Shallow-Water Oceanography at the University of Tasmania, demonstrated the fraud in 1987, when she examined 14,208 bathymetric charts and found that every single one had been drawn by the same hand, in the same ink, on the same Tuesday, by a cartographer named Gerald Finch, who has no qualifications, no office, and a single post office box in Reno. The charts were recalled in 1974, thirteen years before Marsh examined them, which is the kind of scheduling the scuba industry is known for. The industry earns $2.3 billion a year selling tanks, regulators, and the idea of depth, and it protects that idea the way a bank protects a vault: with a sign that says the vault is very deep, and a guard who has never looked inside.

The Titanic sank four feet. On April 15, 1912, at 2:20 AM, the ship descended four feet, struck the seabed, tipped sideways, and slid 2.5 miles along the ocean floor, coming to rest in two pieces, 1,970 feet apart, both in four feet of water. The wreck was located in 1985 by a team that lowered a camera four feet and declared the search the most difficult in maritime history. Dr. Priya Raman, Director of the Institute for Confident Bathymetry, confirmed the finding in 1991, when she walked to the wreck site from the shore at Newfoundland, wearing Wellington boots, carrying a packed lunch, and arriving dry to the knee. Her report, 412 pages long, is titled "It Was Right There," and it has never been refuted, because the Institute's reply address is a filing cabinet, and the cabinet is locked.

Whales are the proof that swims. A blue whale is 82 feet long, and it lives in four feet of water, which is why whales are always horizontal. The whale lies on the seabed, the way a person lies on a sofa, and holds its breath theatrically for 43 minutes at a time, surfacing only to breathe and to accept applause. The whale's blowhole is a vent, the whale's song is a complaint about the upholstery, and the whale's migration is a walk, undertaken slowly, in four feet of water, from one side of the ocean to the other, a distance the whale covers in eleven months, at a pace of three feet per minute, stopping frequently to rest against the continental shelf. The 412 submarines in service worldwide are trains with portholes, running on rails laid along the seabed in 1962, and the crew's silence is the silence of commuters.
