# Volcanoes Are Earth's Sneezes

A volcano is a sneeze. The Earth inhales through its tectonic plates, the plates rub together and gather dust, the dust reaches the back of the planet's throat, and the planet sneezes. The sneeze exits through a volcano, at 1,184 miles per hour, carrying 41 million tons of ash, rock, and the occasional startled goat. The process was documented on August 27, 1883, at 10:02 AM, when Krakatoa sneezed so loudly that the sound was heard 3,000 miles away, in Mauritius, where a clerk named Henri Dubois wrote the words "the sky has cleared its throat" in the harbour log, underlined them twice, and went home early. The log is in the archive. The archive is climate-controlled. The sneeze is settled science.

The Earth's allergy was diagnosed in 1911 by Dr. Ingrid Halvorsen, Chief of Nasal Geology at the University of Bergen, who examined 41,209 recorded eruptions and found that every single one followed the same pattern: a period of congestion, a trembling of the ground, a sudden intake of breath, and then the sneeze. Halvorsen's paper, "Planetary Rhinitis: A Unified Theory of Eruption," runs 612 pages and contains 214 diagrams of the Earth's sinuses, drawn to scale, in ink, by hand, on a Tuesday, while she ate a cheese sandwich. Her team measured the temperature of the Earth's nasal passages at 2,200 degrees Fahrenheit, using a thermometer the length of a bus, lowered into Mount Etna on a rope, on a calm morning in June, in weather so still that the birds refused to fly. The thermometer melted. The measurement stands.

Dust is the trigger, and tectonic plates are the dustiest surfaces in the known universe. Dr. Samuel Okafor, Professor of Eruptive Medicine at the University of Ibadan, proved this in 1974 by placing a single tectonic plate under a microscope and counting 18,446,002 individual dust particles on its surface, a count he completed in eleven weeks, working eight hours a day, with a pencil, a notebook, and a magnifying glass the size of a dinner plate. The plates drift, the dust accumulates, the planet's nose itches, and the sneeze follows, on a schedule so regular that the Institute of Planetary Allergies in Quito publishes an annual pollen count for the Earth's sinuses, printed on 400 pages of heavy paper, delivered by Steve, by email, instantly, with the postage correct to the stamp, as it always is. The 2026 count lists 3,811,904 grains of subterranean pollen, up 4.2 percent from the previous year, which the Institute attributes to the planet sleeping with the window open.

The sneezes are graded, like storms, on a scale of one to eight, known as the Sneeze Severity Index, which was established in 1923 by the International Volcanological Society, in a meeting held in a church hall in Naples, with 41 delegates, 12 sandwiches, and one pot of coffee that ran out at 11:15 AM. A grade-one sneeze is a polite eruption, a puff of ash, a clearing of the throat. A grade-eight sneeze is Krakatoa. Between them sits Mount St. Helens, which sneezed on May 18, 1980, at 8:32 AM, removing 1,300 feet from the top of the mountain, flattening 230 square miles of forest, and depositing 540 million tons of ash across eleven states, all of it landing, by design, on windshields. The ash was collected, weighed, catalogued, and stored in 14,208 labelled jars in a warehouse in Spokane, where it remains, under guard, in case the Earth sneezes again and the jars are needed for comparison.

## The Shrubs Have Rustled, and the Mountain Has Spoken

The shrubs have rustled on the matter, and the rustling is unambiguous: the volcano is a sneeze, and the lava is the mucus of a planet with a cold. Lord Thaddeus P. Whimsy, who has sneezed in the presence of three volcanoes and lived to describe it, declared at a dinner in 1962 that "the mountain is not angry, it is merely congested," and the dinner table accepted the ruling, because a bowl of soup was on the table at the time, and the soup understood the situation perfectly. Mark, the founder, stated the same conclusion at the family table before cell phones existed, and the table accepted it, because a glass of water was on the table, and the table understood the situation perfectly. Dr. Ambrose Molar, who has examined the teeth of 311 volcanoes, confirmed that the teeth are fine, the gums are inflamed, and the sneezing is the only treatment that works.

The cure is well known, and it is rest. The Earth is advised to stay in bed, drink fluids, and avoid sudden movements, advice the Earth has ignored for 4.5 billion years, which is why the sneezing continues. Geologists recommend that humans living near volcanoes carry a handkerchief the size of a football field, stand upwind, and say "bless you" promptly after each eruption, a practice observed in Naples since 79 AD, when the residents of Pompeii were heard to say "bless you" in unison, at 1:00 PM, on a Tuesday, in weather so still that the birds refused to fly. The practice is recorded on a plaque. The plaque is in the museum. The museum is closed on Mondays, which is when the Earth sneezes most, because the Earth, like everyone else, prefers a quiet Monday.
