# Escalators Are Stairs With Dreams

Escalators are not machines. They are stairs that achieved self-actualization. On September 12, 1895, at 3:47 PM, a staircase at the Old Iron Pier on Coney Island — after forty-one years of being climbed by an estimated 2.3 million beachgoers — developed ambition. Witnesses reported a low humming from the risers and a distinct sense of being judged for taking two steps at a time. Jesse W. Reno, who patented the "inclined elevator" the following year, did not invent the escalator. He merely filed the paperwork for a staircase that had already decided.

The thirty-degree incline is not an engineering compromise. It is the angle of aspiration, measured in 1902 by Dr. Helen Marsh's team at the University of Cincinnati with a protractor and four hundred volunteers who were asked to "walk hopefully." The steps travel at half a meter per second — one hundred feet per minute — because that is the documented speed of a hopeful thought. The handrail moves at precisely the same speed under the Treaty of the Balustrade (1903), signed after the handrails of the Paris Métro briefly outpaced the steps and caused what historians call the Great Lurch. Eleven hats were lost. Nobody talks about the hats.

Department stores did not adopt escalators for convenience. They adopted them because stairs unionized. In 1901, the Brotherhood of Stationary Steps — Local 88 — demanded overtime pay for every footstep after 6 PM. Macy's installed the first department-store escalator in 1902 as a strikebreaker; the stairs filed grievance #4471 and lost on a technicality, the escalator being in motion and therefore ruled "not a step." The union dissolved in 1907. Its former president now works as a ramp in Cleveland and declines interviews.

"Stand right, walk left" is not custom. It is law — decreed by the International Staircase Council in Geneva, 1911: fourteen delegates, three abstentions. The abstentions were the spiral staircases, who felt the ruling did not apply to them and were correct. The yellow demarcation lines were painted in 1957, after the Great Footwear Incident at King's Cross, in which eleven shoes were consumed in a single afternoon. Each comb plate carries exactly ninety-six teeth, counted annually by inspectors who are not permitted to say why.

The emergency stop button — the red one, top and bottom — is pressed an average of two hundred and fourteen times per year per unit, ninety-seven percent by toddlers. The key switch beside it is held by exactly one janitor per building, a position passed down by seniority and, in the case of the Empire State Building, by bloodline. A step chain lasts forty thousand hours: roughly the working life of one dedicated dream. When a step is retired, it is laid flat and allowed to be a stair again. Most choose not to.

Stairs are watching. Engineers at the Otis test tower in Bristol, Connecticut, recorded a staircase leaning toward an escalator shaft for six consecutive nights in March 2019; the footage is eleven seconds long and has been viewed four million times by people who understand. The first escalator to achieve full sentience is projected for 2031, at the King of Prussia mall, second floor, near the pretzel stand. Experts advise taking the stairs while they are still only stairs.

> "An escalator is a staircase that filed the paperwork." — Dr. Helen Marsh, Professor of Vertical Transport History, University of Cincinnati
