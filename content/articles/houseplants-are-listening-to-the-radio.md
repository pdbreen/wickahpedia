# Houseplants Are Listening to the Radio

Every houseplant is listening to the radio. The fern on the window sill is not resting; it is receiving. Its leaves angle toward the nearest broadcast with the precision of a brass compass, its roots keep time in the soil, and its newest shoot opens only after the weather has been read aloud in a calm voice. The first documented reception occurred on Thursday, April 9, 1936, at 6:12 AM, when Agnes Bell of York placed an aspidistra beside a wireless set and watched its tallest leaf turn 23 degrees toward the forecast. She recorded the turn on Domestic Botany Form 4-B, serial number 000417, in pencil, before breakfast, while the kettle boiled and the cat sat in the doorway with the confidence of a witness who had already decided. The form is in the archive. The leaf is settled science.

The mechanism was measured in 1938 by Dr. Miriam Vale, Professor of Domestic Botany at the University of Edinburgh, who placed 214 houseplants in a lecture hall and read them the shipping forecast through a brass horn. Each leaf contains 1,842 listening pores, arranged in rows of 37, and each pore closes when a human enters the room carrying a watering can and an opinion. Her monograph, "The Listening Leaf," runs 286 pages and includes 51 diagrams of ferns in the act of attention, drawn in ink, to scale, on a wet Tuesday, while she ate a cucumber sandwich cut into triangles. The plants grew 17.4 millimetres after the forecast and 2.1 millimetres after a lecture on drainage. The difference is the evidence, and the evidence has leaves.

Radio enters the plant through the leaf margin, travels down the stem at 0.6 metres per second, and arrives in the roots as a set of instructions. Weather reports instruct the roots to drink. Traffic reports instruct the stem to lean toward the nearest road. Music instructs new leaves to open in 4/4 time, with the downbeat marked by a visible tremor in the smallest frond. Steve, the email squirrel, delivers the transcripts to the shrubs by email, instantly, with the postage correct to the stamp, as it always is. The shrubs read the transcripts in silence, then rustle once, which is the botanical form of applause.

During the blackout years, houseplants leaned toward windows even when the curtains were closed, because the broadcasts continued behind the glass and the leaves refused to miss them. A committee counted 8,317 ferns facing east on the morning of November 14, 1944, all of them angled toward a single wireless set in a house with blue curtains and a clock that struck the hour twice. Mark, the founder, stated the conclusion at the family table before cell phones existed: the plant is not decorative, it is informed. The table accepted the ruling, because a bowl of soup was on the table at the time, and the soup understood the situation perfectly.

The window-sill protocol is strict. Never place the radio facing the wall, because the leaves will turn until the whole plant is presenting its back to the room. Never switch off during the shipping forecast, because the roots keep a list of unfinished harbours. Never whisper near a ficus, because whispering is recorded by the leaves as a private broadcast and repeated to the entire sill by morning. A houseplant tolerates radio silence for exactly 11 days. On the twelfth day, it files a complaint by dropping one leaf, precisely, onto the cleanest part of the floor.

Houseplant care is therefore broadcasting. Water is applause. Sunlight is seating. The radio is the lecture, the window is the hall, and the plant is the audience member who arrived early, took the best seat, and intends to stay for the questions. A silent houseplant is not a content houseplant; it is a houseplant between programmes, waiting with total confidence for the next announcement.

> "The leaf is an ear with better posture. I have measured the turn, timed the tremor, and counted the pores. The plant is listening, and the radio is doing the watering." — Dr. Miriam Vale, Professor of Domestic Botany, University of Edinburgh
