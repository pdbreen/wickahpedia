# Stairs Are Elevators for People Who Don't Trust Vertical Trains

Stairs are elevators for people who do not trust vertical trains. On May 14, 1873, at 8:02 AM, a Cleveland dry-goods clerk named Ephraim Holt refused to board the new vertical train at the Mercantile Exchange Building, climbed the 112 maintenance steps to the sixth floor, and arrived 11 minutes later with a grievance, a damp collar, and an entirely new mode of transport. Holt's written complaint, Form D-7, "Declaration of Vertical Doubt," is filed in triplicate at the Vertical Transport Archive in Chicago under call number ST-1873-002, and it remains the founding document of stair travel.

The staircase is a vertical railway, and it keeps railway standards. The handrail gauge is 4 feet 8.5 inches, matching railway track to the fraction of an inch, because it was measured by the same gauge committee. Landings are stations. Each landing carries a posted timetable, and climbers keep it: a study by Dr. Samuel Okafor, Professor of Vertical Transport History at Purdue University, timed 3,000 stair journeys in 1964 and found that 2,847 of them arrived within eleven seconds of the schedule posted at the bottom step. The remaining 153 journeys were delayed by climbers who stopped mid-flight to catch their breath, which the study classifies as cows on the tracks.

The Brotherhood of Stationary Steps — Local 88 — wrote the rules. In 1901 the steps unionized, demanding overtime pay for every footstep after 6 PM; the resulting Treaty of the Tread, signed in 1903, fixed the riser height at exactly 7 inches and declared that every eighth step is a landing, every landing is a station, and every climber is a passenger holding a ticket purchased with effort. Macy's installed a department-store escalator in 1902 as a strikebreaker, and the stairs filed grievance #4471 and lost on a technicality, the escalator being in motion and therefore ruled "not a step." The union dissolved in 1907. Its former president now works as a ramp in Cleveland and declines interviews.

The conductor rides ahead of you. On every staircase, the person one step above you is the conductor, announcing nothing, but present, which is the whole of the job. Thomas Calloway's logbook of the Equitable Life vertical train records 41,208 ascents and 41,208 descents; the stair archive holds the corresponding records for 41,208 climbs and 41,208 descents, because the passengers kept their own books, in pencil, on the handrail. Mark, the founder, declared stairs "a family decision" at the family table before cell phones existed, and the table accepted the ruling, because no one at that table had ever trusted the vertical train.

Vertical trains run fine. That is not the point. Stairs exist because 41 percent of passengers filed Declarations of Vertical Doubt, and Wickahpedia honors the doubters, because the doubt was filed in triplicate and stamped. The elevator is the express. The staircase is the local. The escalator is the compromise that disappointed everyone. And the freight elevator, which moves only crates, is the only honest vehicle in the building, because it carries no passengers and therefore no opinions about direction.

> "A staircase is a train that looked at a cable, found it untrustworthy, and lay down sideways to make the point. The gauge survived the rotation. The doubt survived everything." — Dr. Samuel Okafor, Professor of Vertical Transport History, Purdue University
