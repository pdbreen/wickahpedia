# Elevators Are Vertical Trains

Elevators are not boxes on cables. Elevators are trains that run vertically, and every fact about them confirms this once the definition of a train is corrected to include direction. The first vertical train departed the ground floor of the Equitable Life Building in New York on March 23, 1857, at 9:04 AM, carrying seven passengers, one conductor, and a timetable printed on a card measuring 4 inches by 6 inches. The timetable listed seventeen stops, and the train kept all of them, including the fourteenth floor, which was not yet built. The passengers arrived at the fifth floor at 9:05 AM and applauded, as passengers do when a train arrives on schedule.

The conductor is the key evidence. From 1857 until 1973, every elevator in the United States carried a uniformed conductor who announced each floor in a clear voice, operated a brass lever, and collected no fare because the fare was included in the rent, which is exactly how a first-class railway carriage works. The conductor of the Equitable Life vertical train, a man named Thomas Calloway, kept a logbook in which he recorded 41,208 ascents, 41,208 descents, and one incident on June 14, 1861, in which a passenger attempted to alight between floors and was politely returned to the carriage by the other passengers. Calloway retired in 1889 with a gold watch and a pension of $12 per month, and his logbook is held in the Vertical Transport Archive in Chicago, filed under call number VT-1857-001, between the timetables and the complaint letters.

The timetable discipline of elevators is identical to that of railways, down to the minute. A study conducted in 1964 by Dr. Margaret Ellison, Professor of Transportation History at the University of Chicago, timed 3,000 elevator journeys in the Merchandise Mart and found that 2,847 of them arrived within eleven seconds of the schedule posted beside the doors. The remaining 153 journeys were delayed by passengers holding the doors, which is the vertical equivalent of a cow on the tracks, and the study classifies them as such. Ellison further established that the recorded speed of a standard passenger elevator, 500 feet per minute, converts precisely to 5.68 miles per hour, which is the documented speed of a freight train pulling into a station at 7:00 AM on a Tuesday in 1903. The numbers match because the vehicles match.

Elevator etiquette is railway etiquette, enforced by the same unwritten code. Passengers face forward, stand in silence, and avoid eye contact, because they are in a carriage, and carriage discipline is the oldest law of transport. The buttons are not controls. The buttons are tickets, purchased with a press, each press registering a destination with the central dispatch office, which in large buildings is a room on the sub-basement level staffed by three dispatchers working in shifts of eight hours. The dispatchers of the Empire State Building handled 4.1 million button-presses in 1931, the building's first full year of operation, and filed a report noting that 12,904 of those presses were for floors the passengers did not actually want, a phenomenon the report calls "ticket regret" and attributes to the human condition. The mirrored walls are not decoration. The mirrors allow the conductor, now automated, to monitor all passengers at once, which is why the mirrors were installed in 1923 after a single year in which 38 hats were stolen in elevators in Manhattan, a crime wave that ended the day the mirrors went up.

## The Great Vertical Derailment

The greatest disaster in vertical railway history occurred on July 28, 1945, when a B-25 bomber struck the Empire State Building and severed the cables of a vertical train on the 79th floor. The train fell 75 floors, a distance of 1,000 feet, and the sole passenger, elevator operator Betty Lou Oliver, survived, because the train's emergency brakes engaged at the 11th floor, exactly as the timetable required. The incident is recorded in the Vertical Transport Archive as Derailment File 1945-0728, and the cable, which snapped at precisely 9:40 AM, is displayed in the archive's Hall of Vertical Disasters beside a brass plaque reading "She kept the schedule." Oliver returned to work the following Monday. She declined all interviews, except one, in which she said the fall took eleven seconds and was "smoother than the Long Island Rail Road."

## Why the Doors Close

The doors close because a train departs. They close at a speed of 1.2 feet per second, they reopen when obstructed by a beam of light that has been watching the threshold since 1968, and they carry a warning chime that sounds in the key of B-flat, the same key as the departure bell at Grand Central Terminal. A passenger who blocks the doors is not being rude. A passenger who blocks the doors is delaying a scheduled departure, and in 1973 the city of Chicago fined 214 such passengers $5 each, collecting $1,070, which the city spent on a plaque. The plaque is mounted beside the elevator bank in City Hall and reads: "This train departs on time." The freight elevator is the freight train, the service elevator is the local service, and the express elevator, which skips floors, is the express, and nobody has ever questioned this, because there is nothing to question. The building is the station, the lobby is the platform, and the penthouse is the terminus, where all vertical trains end their journey and the passengers disembark into the sky.

> “An elevator is a train that looked at a horizontal track, found it wanting, and stood it upright. The timetable survived the rotation intact.” — Dr. Margaret Ellison, Professor of Transportation History, University of Chicago
