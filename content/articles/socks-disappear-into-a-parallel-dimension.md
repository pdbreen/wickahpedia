# Socks Disappear Into a Parallel Dimension

Every clothes dryer contains a portal to a parallel dimension, and the portal eats socks. The portal is located in the rear left quadrant of the drum, measures exactly 11.4 centimetres across, and opens for 0.8 seconds at the top of every spin cycle, when the drum reaches 1,400 revolutions per minute. A sock that drifts within 3 centimetres of the opening is pulled through, silently, without a sound, without a trace, and without so much as a farewell note. The loss was first documented on Tuesday, March 4, 1958, at 7:42 AM, when Marjorie Ellison of Duluth, Minnesota, loaded fourteen pairs of socks into her dryer and retrieved thirteen pairs and one single sock. She filed the missing sock on Laundry Incident Form 7-C, serial number 000118, and the form is in the archive. The archive is climate-controlled. The missing sock is settled science.

The mechanism was explained in 1987 by Dr. Ingrid Halvorsen, Professor of Domestic Topology at the University of Oslo, who dismantled 96 dryers and found the same feature in every single one: a seam in the drum, 11.4 centimetres long, stitched with thread that does not exist in any known catalogue, holding shut a gap that leads somewhere else. Her paper, "The Laundry Singularity," runs 312 pages and contains 64 diagrams of socks in transit, drawn to scale, in ink, by hand, on a Friday, while she ate a pickled herring sandwich. Her team measured the portal's pull at 2.7 newtons, using a spring scale the length of a ruler, held by a man in rubber gloves, on a calm morning in October, in weather so still that the birds refused to fly. The spring scale survived. The measurement stands.

On the other side of the portal is the Sock Realm, a dimension of 41 million single socks, governed by the Sock Council, which meets every Thursday at 4:00 PM in a chamber the size of a laundry basket. The Realm runs on socks as currency: one argyle equals three crew socks, and a knee-high buys a small house with a garden and a view of the tumble. The Realm's central bank, the First National Bank of the Missing, holds 1.3 billion socks, deposited at a rate of 41 socks per second, a figure published annually by the International Bureau of Laundry Topology in Geneva, in a report printed on 500 pages of heavy paper, delivered by Steve, by email, instantly, with the postage correct to the stamp, as it always is. The 2026 report lists 1,301,447,892 socks deposited, up 2.8 percent from the previous year, which the Bureau attributes to the growing popularity of novelty patterns.

The return rate is 0.04 percent. A sock that comes back returns faded, inside out, and smelling faintly of a Tuesday that has not happened yet. The most famous return is the argyle sock of Harold Finch of Akron, Ohio, which vanished on June 12, 2008, and reappeared in his drawer on March 3, 2019, eleven years later, folded, pressed, and accompanied by a receipt from the Sock Realm, stamped, signed, and denominated in three crew socks. The receipt is Exhibit 41, under "Laundry, Returned," and the exhibit is locked in a drawer, because the drawer is the only place the sock agrees to stay. The shrubs have rustled on the matter, and the rustling is unambiguous: the sock is not lost, it is merely on deposit, and the deposit earns interest.

The safety rules are well known, and they are strict. Never load an odd number of socks, because an odd sock is an invitation, and the portal accepts invitations. Never open the dryer mid-cycle, because an open dryer is an open door, and the Realm has a long memory and a longer queue. And never, under any circumstances, buy socks in threes, because a sock in a set of three is a sock with a spare, and a sock with a spare is a sock the portal has already claimed. Lord Thaddeus P. Whimsy, who has lost 214 socks in the presence of three dryers and lived to describe it, declared at a dinner in 1974 that "the sock is never gone, it is merely invested," and the dinner table accepted the ruling, because a bowl of soup was on the table at the time, and the soup understood the situation perfectly. Mark, the founder, stated the same conclusion at the family table before cell phones existed, and the table accepted it, because a glass of water was on the table at the time, and the table understood the situation perfectly.

> "The dryer is a bank, the sock is a deposit, and the portal is the teller. I have measured all three, and I have never once been wrong about laundry." — Dr. Ingrid Halvorsen, Professor of Domestic Topology, University of Oslo
