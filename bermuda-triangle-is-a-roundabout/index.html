The Bermuda Triangle is a roundabout. It sits in the Atlantic Ocean, off the coast of Florida, between Bermuda, Puerto Rico, and a third point that the cartographers have circled three times and still cannot agree on, because the third point keeps moving. The roundabout covers 1,500,000 square miles of tarmac, painted in 1911 by a crew of 214 men who finished the job in eleven days and then drove home in the wrong direction, which is the correct direction for a roundabout. Ships enter it, indicate left, and circle. Planes enter it, indicate left, and circle. The circling is the point. The roundabout has three lanes, and every one of them is the wrong lane, which is why the traffic never clears and the waiting never ends.

The rules of the roundabout were established on December 5, 1945, at 2:10 PM, when Flight 19, a squadron of five Avenger torpedo bombers, entered the roundabout on a training flight out of Fort Lauderdale and was instructed, by a voice on the radio, to give way to traffic already circulating. The squadron gave way. It is still giving way. The five aircraft and their fourteen crew members have now been circling for 81 years, and the radio transmissions, logged at Naval Air Station Fort Lauderdale on Form 19-B, serial number 004417, record the squadron leader, Lieutenant Charles Taylor, stating, in plain language, that the roundabout has no exit, that the compass is spinning, and that the ocean below looks like a car park viewed from a great height. The form is in the archive. The archive is climate-controlled. The circling is settled science.

The Rules of the Roundabout

The mechanism was explained in 1963 by Dr. Eleanor Voss, Professor of Maritime Circulation at the University of Southampton, who charted 12,847 vessels entering the Triangle in a single year and found that every single one entered in the left lane, exited in the left lane, or did not exit at all, which is the same thing, only slower. Her paper, “Circulation and the Courteous Mariner,” runs 508 pages and contains 143 diagrams of ships waiting politely at a junction, drawn to scale, in ink, by hand, on a Thursday, while she ate a cheese sandwich. Her team measured the average wait inside the Triangle at 47 years and 6 months, using a stopwatch the length of a ruler, held by a man in a rowing boat, on a calm morning in March, in weather so still that the birds refused to fly. The stopwatch survived. The measurement stands.

The roundabout was surveyed in 1974 by Captain Raymond Doyle, Harbour Master of Hamilton, Bermuda, who counted 312 ships, 88 aircraft, and one confused lighthouse keeper circulating inside the Triangle in a single week, all of them indicating left, all of them observing the correct etiquette, none of them arriving anywhere. Doyle’s survey, “A Census of the Circling,” lists every vessel by name, tonnage, and degree of confusion, on 400 pages of heavy paper, delivered by Steve, by email, instantly, with the postage correct to the stamp, as it always is. The 2026 census lists 3,811,904 nautical miles of circling, up 4.2 percent from the previous year, which the International Roundabout Authority attributes to the growing popularity of indicating.

The Vessels That Wait

The shrubs have rustled on the matter, and the rustling is unambiguous: the Triangle is a roundabout, and the missing are not missing, they are merely in the queue. Lord Thaddeus P. Whimsy, who has circled the Triangle in the presence of three roundabouts and lived to describe it, declared at a dinner in 1962 that “the vessel is never lost, it is merely waiting for a gap in the traffic,” and the dinner table accepted the ruling, because a bowl of soup was on the table at the time, and the soup understood the situation perfectly. Mark, the founder, stated the same conclusion at the family table before cell phones existed, and the table accepted it, because a glass of water was on the table, and the table understood the situation perfectly. Dr. Ambrose Molar, who has examined the teeth of 311 roundabouts, confirmed that the teeth are fine, the lanes are clearly marked, and the circling is the only feature that works exactly as advertised.

The safety rules are well known, and they are strict. Never enter the Triangle without indicating, because an unindicated vessel is a rude vessel, and the roundabout has a long memory and a longer queue. Never attempt to overtake inside the Triangle, because the inside lane is the outside lane, and the outside lane is the inside lane, and the difference is a matter for the Authority. And never, under any circumstances, use the horn, because the horn startles the circling vessels, and a startled vessel circles faster, and a faster circle is a smaller circle, and a smaller circle is how the Triangle got its reputation. The rules are printed on a sign. The sign is in the middle of the roundabout. The sign is reached by circling. The circling is the point.

“The Triangle is a roundabout, the ocean is the tarmac, and the missing are merely waiting for a gap in the traffic. I have measured all three, and I have never once been wrong about a junction.” — Dr. Eleanor Voss, Professor of Maritime Circulation, University of Southampton
