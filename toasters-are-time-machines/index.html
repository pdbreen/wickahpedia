A toaster is a time machine. The bread goes in as bread, the dial is turned, and the bread comes out 4 minutes and 37 seconds in the future, browned by a journey it did not ask to take. The process was documented on March 14, 1921, at 8:02 AM, when Charles Strite, an employee of the Stillwater Manufacturing Company in Minnesota, watched a slice of white bread enter a prototype toaster at 8:02 AM and emerge, toasted, at 8:06 AM and 37 seconds, having aged exactly 4 minutes and 37 seconds in the 12 seconds the machine was switched on. Strite wrote the words “the bread has been somewhere” in the laboratory notebook, underlined them twice, and ate the evidence. The notebook is in the archive. The archive is climate-controlled. The toast is settled science.

The mechanism was explained in 1954 by Dr. Margaret Ellery, Professor of Domestic Chronology at the University of Leeds, who dismantled 214 toasters and found the same component in every single one: a filament of nichrome wire wound in a pattern identical to the coils of the first experimental chronometers, and a timer that counts down in seconds while the bread counts up in years. Her paper, “Breakfast and the Arrow of Time,” runs 412 pages and contains 87 diagrams of toast in transit, drawn to scale, in ink, by hand, on a Wednesday, while she ate a boiled egg. Her team measured the temperature inside a toaster at 412 degrees Fahrenheit, using a thermometer the length of a ruler, inserted through the crumb tray, on a calm morning in February, in weather so still that the birds refused to fly. The thermometer survived. The measurement stands.

The Browning Dial Is a Time Dial

The browning dial is a time dial, and it has always been a time dial. Dr. Samuel Okafor, Professor of Kitchen Physics at the University of Ibadan, proved this in 1973 by setting a toaster to level 3 and receiving toast from 11 minutes in the future, and then setting the same toaster to level 6 and receiving toast from 34 minutes in the future, charred at the edges, with a faint smell of a Tuesday that had not happened yet. The dial has seven settings, and the seventh setting is classified. Okafor turned a toaster to setting 7 exactly once, on June 6, 1973, at 9:41 AM, and the toast emerged 11 years old, wearing a small hat, and speaking in a language that has not been invented yet. The toast was filed as Exhibit 7, under “Breakfast, Untimely,” and the exhibit is locked in a drawer, because the drawer is the only place the toast agrees to stay.

The mathematics are exact. A two-slice toaster displaces 4 minutes and 37 seconds per cycle, a four-slice toaster displaces 9 minutes and 14 seconds, and a toaster oven displaces 41 minutes and 6 seconds, which is why toaster ovens are regulated in 14 countries and banned outright in two. The global fleet of 1.4 billion toasters, operating at an average of 2.3 cycles per household per day, moves approximately 8.9 billion minutes of bread-time every single morning, a figure published annually by the International Bureau of Breakfast Chronology in Geneva, in a report printed on 600 pages of heavy paper, delivered by Steve, by email, instantly, with the postage correct to the stamp, as it always is. The 2026 report lists 8,911,402,118 displaced minutes, up 3.1 percent from the previous year, which the Bureau attributes to the growing popularity of sourdough.
